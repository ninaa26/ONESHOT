01

What this thing actually is

Cornell's Autonomous Sailboat Team builds robot sailboats. Testing one on real water is slow and expensive — you need wind, a lake, and a day. So they wrote a program that pretends to be a sailboat.

You give sailbench a wind, a rudder angle, and a sail setting. It calculates what a real boat would do and reports the new position twenty milliseconds later. Repeat fifty times a second and you have a sailing simulation.

It gets used two ways:

  • As a game. You steer with the arrow keys in a browser, which sanity-checks that the physics feels like sailing.
  • As a training gym. An AI learns to sail by trying hundreds of thousands of times in fast-forward, with no boat to crash and no lake to book.

The "bench" is short for benchmark — a standard rig you measure things against. It is now about 10,600 lines of Python and 8,700 of JavaScript, which is more than an afternoon’s read — but it is built so that you rarely need to read all of it. Almost everything is a part in a slot, and section 04 is the key to that.

Orientation

Five directories matter. sailbench/ is the physics and the RL environment. configs/ holds YAML files describing boats — this is the tuning surface, and you can change the boat completely without touching code. web/ is four browser front ends over a shared core (section 08). scripts/ holds the command-line tools, including the CAD measurement pipeline that produced the real boat’s numbers. runs/ holds trained neural networks, including several that already work.

02

The sailing physics you need first

This section contains no code. Skip it and the code will not make sense.

How does a boat sail toward the wind?

The genuinely confusing thing about sailing is that boats can travel at an angle into the wind. That seems impossible — the wind is pushing you backwards. Here is the resolution.

A sail is not a parachute. A sail is a wing, turned vertical. A wing doesn't work by being pushed; it works by bending the air around it. Air flowing over the curved side travels further and faster, which drops its pressure, and the wing gets sucked toward the low pressure. That sideways suck is called lift.

Critically: lift acts perpendicular to the airflow, not along it. So a correctly angled sail generates a force pointing mostly sideways, not downwind. That is the whole trick.

But sideways isn't forward either

Correct — and this is where the second wing comes in. Under the boat hangs a keel: a fin sticking straight down. The keel is also a wing, but it works in water, and water is about 800 times denser than air.

Now you have two forces. The sail pushes sideways-and-slightly-forward through the air. The keel resists sideways very hard through the water. The sideways parts largely cancel, and what survives is the forward part.

The boat squirts forward like a wet watermelon seed squeezed between two fingers. That is sailing. The keel is the entire reason a sailboat isn't just a leaf blowing downwind.

Apparent wind: the wind the sail actually feels

Stick your hand out of a car window on a perfectly still day and you feel wind that isn't there. A moving boat does the same. The sail never responds to the true wind — it responds to the apparent wind, which is the true wind minus your own velocity.

This matters constantly, because it changes as you accelerate. Speed up and the apparent wind swings forward and strengthens, which changes the correct sail angle, which changes your speed. Sailing is a feedback loop, and this is where the loop closes.

The no-go zone, and why boats zigzag

There is a cone of roughly 45° either side of straight-into-the-wind where no sail angle produces useful lift. The sail just flaps. This is the no-go zone, and it is a hard physical limit, not a skill issue.

To reach a point inside that cone, you sail as close to the edge of it as you can on one side, then turn and do the same on the other. That is tacking. It is also exactly the task the reinforcement-learning agent in this repo is trained to solve.

03

How the boat is represented in code

The State: seven numbers

Everything the simulator knows about the boat at one instant lives in sailbench/models/model.py:

x, y   position in the world             (metres)
psi    heading, stored as (cos, sin)     (a direction, not an angle)
u      surge  — speed along the nose     (m/s)
v      sway   — speed out the side       (m/s)
r      yaw rate — how fast it spins      (rad/s)

Why store the heading as a cosine/sine pair rather than "37 degrees"? Because angles wrap around. 359° and 1° are two degrees apart, but numerically they look 358 apart, and arithmetic across that seam produces garbage. A point on the unit circle has no seam. The code re-normalises the pair every step, because floating-point rounding slowly nudges it off the circle.

Why u and v in boat coordinates rather than world velocity? Because forces are naturally boat-relative. The keel resists sideways motion, and "sideways" only means anything relative to the boat. The sim works in boat coordinates and converts to world coordinates only when it needs to draw something.

Terminology

Surge is forward/back, sway is left/right, yaw is turning. These are the standard naval-architecture names for the degrees of freedom, and using them means this code reads like the marine-engineering literature it draws on.

Reference frames and the transform tree

This is the one genuinely elegant piece of architecture in the repo. Five different points of view are in play at once, and every force calculation happens in one of them but must be reported in another.

The sail computes lift in the airflow frame, needs it in the sail frame, then in the boat frame so it can be added to everything else. Doing that by hand with raw trigonometry is precisely where simulator bugs breed.

Instead of hand-rolled trigonometry, TFTree2D stores frames as a family tree and exposes one function:

tf_tree.vector_to_frame(force, "sail", "boat")

"Take this arrow, which I measured in sail-land, and tell me what it looks like in boat-land." The tree walks up to the root and back down, composing rotation matrices. TF is short for transform.

04

The four forces

Four components make forces, a fifth drags on everything above water, and the hub adds them up. Which model fills each role is section 07 — this section is what they all have in common.

Every component implements exactly one method — compute(state, tf_tree) returning forces in newtons in the boat frame. That uniform interface is why the hub can simply loop over them and sum the results — and it is also why a component can be swapped for a different one without anything else noticing.

The wing formula

Sail, keel and rudder are all wings, so they share a parent class, Foil. The core of it:

Lift = CL × ½ρv² × Area
Drag = CD × ½ρv² × Area
SymbolNameWhat it means
ρDensityAir is 1.225 kg/m³, water is 1000. The ~800× ratio is why a small keel balances a huge sail.
vFlow speedHow fast fluid moves past the wing.
½ρv²Dynamic pressureThe "punch" of moving fluid. Note the square — double the wind, quadruple the force.
AreaPlanform areaBigger wing, more force. Set per component in the YAML.
CL, CDCoefficientsDimensionless numbers capturing the wing's shape efficiency. Everything about the cross-section is baked in here.

Angle of attack, and stall

Angle of attack is the angle between the wing's chord line and the oncoming flow. It is the single most important variable in the whole simulator, because CL and CD are functions of it.

Where do CL and CD come from? Two of the dependencies you installed. aerosandbox is an aerodynamics toolkit, and neuralfoil is a small neural network trained on wing simulations. Rather than solving fluid equations — seconds per query — it predicts CL and CD instantly.

That speed is not a nicety. The sim queries it four times per step, fifty steps per simulated second, across four parallel training environments, for 750,000 steps. Real fluid solving would take months.

Reading the config

NACA0012 is a standardised wing shape catalogued by the US aeronautics body in the 1930s: symmetric, 12% as thick as it is long. Reynolds number (res: [5e5]) is a dimensionless ratio of momentum to stickiness that tells you whether flow is smooth or churning. Here it is hardcoded rather than computed from actual speed — one of several deliberate simplifications.

The four components

ComponentRoleThe interesting part
Sail Makes the driving force Builds true wind, subtracts boat velocity for apparent wind, rotates into the sail frame to get angle of attack, then applies a luffing ramp: below luff_deg the sail is flapping and lift is smoothly faded to zero. The ramp is deliberate — a hard cutoff would make forces jump discontinuously and destabilise the solver.
Keel Resists sideways slide Fixed along the hull, so its angle of attack is the leeway angle — the mismatch between where the boat points and where it actually moves. If the boat slides sideways at all, the keel automatically develops lift opposing that slide. Leeway is self-correcting.
Rudder Steers Its inflow includes a spin term (u − r·y, v + r·x) because the rudder sits 0.3 m aft of centre and swings sideways when the boat rotates. Its force is then multiplied by effectiveness: 0.2 — a fivefold reduction whose comment reads "avoid over-steer."
Hull Slows you down Pure drag, no lift, all three terms quadratic and negative. Written as -k * u * abs(u) rather than u² so the sign survives — drag must oppose motion whichever way you are going. It alone also returns a yaw moment, resisting spin.
Which sail model, and when

Everything above describes the basic sail, and it is still exactly the right model for some boats — WPI’s SailBot carries a rigid wingsail, which is far closer to a NACA section than to a soft sail, and its config picks basic deliberately.

For a soft cambered sail it is the wrong shape, and that is what the ORC models are for. orc_main and orc_w_jib use the ORC VPP coefficient envelope: coefficients tabulated against apparent wind angle, with drive and heel resolved in a single step rather than through chained rotations. Luffing falls out of the trim parameter rather than needing the bolted-on ramp, and a cambered rig makes lift at zero incidence, which a symmetric section cannot.

Neither replaced the other. They are two models in one slot, and a boat’s config says which it means.

Adding it up: forces and torques

The hub loops over all four, sums the forces, and computes each one's torque about the boat's centre:

mz = x_pos * fy - y_pos * fx + mz_direct

Torque is force times lever arm. A force applied at the centre just pushes; the same force applied 0.3 m behind the centre also twists. That is exactly why the rudder steers — it is mounted at x_pos: -0.3, so its sideways force becomes rotation. Move it to x_pos: 0.0 in the YAML and steering stops working entirely.

The output of the whole force stage is three numbers: Fx, Fy, Mz. Push forward, push sideways, twist.

05

Turning forces into motion

Forces give you acceleration. You want position. Getting from one to the other is numerical integration.

The Coriolis terms

du = fx / m + r * v
dv = fy / m - r * u

The F/m part is plain Newton. The r*v and r*u terms exist because we are working inside the boat's own rotating frame. When your coordinate system spins, straight-line motion looks curved from inside it — the same reason a thrown ball appears to curve on a spinning roundabout. These are Coriolis terms, and leaving them out makes every turn wrong.

Why RK4 and not the obvious thing

The naive approach is new = old + velocity × dt, which assumes velocity holds constant across the step. It doesn't, so error accumulates and the simulation drifts or explodes.

Runge–Kutta 4th order instead samples the rate of change four times per step and takes a weighted average:

k1 = f(state)                  # slope at the start
k2 = f(state + dt*k1/2)        # slope at the midpoint, using k1
k3 = f(state + dt*k2/2)        # midpoint again, refined
k4 = f(state + dt*k3)          # slope at the end

state + dt*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)/6

Four times the computation, but error shrinks with the fourth power of step size rather than the first. Halve dt and error drops 16-fold, not 2-fold. The midpoints get double weight because they best represent the interval. This is the standard workhorse of physics simulation.

The sheet model

One design decision is worth understanding on its own. When you press the up or down arrow you are not setting the sail's angle. You are setting a sheet limit — the furthest the sail is allowed to swing.

The hub then decides where the sail actually ends up: it weathervanes, swinging freely to align with the apparent wind, until the rope stops it.

boom_mag = min(free_mag, sheet_limit)

That is exactly how a real sailboat works, and it is why sailing has skill in it. You are managing a constraint, not commanding a position.

The rudder gets its own realism layer: a deadband so tiny inputs are ignored, a max_rate_deg_s because a servo cannot teleport, and a center_tau_s that eases it back to centre exponentially when you let go.

Flingo Floty — the measured boat

The Full Boat Assembly SolidWorks package: 61 parts and 11 sub-assemblies. When this section was first written the files could only be read as a parts list, because they are in SolidWorks’ native format.

What sailbench already gets right

Worth stating, because the gap list is long and the foundations are genuinely sound:

  • RK4 integration with the Coriolis terms included — the rotating-frame terms are frequently omitted in student simulators, and leaving them out makes every turn wrong.
  • Apparent wind computed properly from true wind minus boat velocity.
  • The sheet-limit model. The sail weathervanes freely and the sheet acts as a geometric stop. That is how a real sailboat works, and it is a more sophisticated choice than commanding a sail angle directly.
  • Rudder inflow includes the yaw-rate term (u − r·y, v + r·x) — a genuinely fine detail, since the rudder swings sideways when the boat rotates.
  • The transform tree. Borrowed from ROS, and the right structure for exactly the ROS 2 integration the team wants.

The one omission that dominates the rest

Heel

A keelboat's entire behaviour is a balance between sail force heeling it over and ballast righting it. Heeling spills wind — the sail's effective force falls roughly with the cosine of heel angle — which is the boat's natural gust response and the reason it does not simply capsize in a puff. It also tilts the keel, reducing its lift and increasing leeway.

In 3 DOF none of this exists. Sail force translates into forward drive with no penalty however hard the wind blows. A policy trained here has never encountered the main thing that limits a real sailboat. It is also why the bulb — a large, deliberate lump of ballast that the team designed and built — has no representation at all.

Getting the real numbers out — done, and how

This was the single highest-value action available, and it has been taken. The recipe, for the next boat:

  • Mass properties (Evaluate → Mass Properties) on the top-level assembly gives total mass, centre of gravity, and the full inertia tensor. That replaces mass: 27.0 and inertia_z: 10.0 with measured values, and supplies the roll inertia any future heel model will need.
  • A STEP export of the hull makes real hydrostatics possible — displacement, waterline, righting-arm curve — instead of the L/B/T box approximation the drag model uses now.
  • Sail areas and centroids from the main and jib sketches give the real areas and centres of effort, rather than the single area: 3.0 currently in the YAML.
  • Keel and rudder planform areas, and the rudder's distance aft of the CG — the lever arm that makes steering work at all.

None of this required changing the simulator. It replaced guessed constants with measured ones, and it is the cheapest accuracy available anywhere in this project. The sail is the one that resisted: it is nine triangles of open surface in the STL with no measurable volume, so area, rig height and centre of effort were taken from the sail plan instead — main 1.196 m² plus jib 0.775 m², which is why Flingo is sailed as a sloop rather than a single sail.

WPI Wild Goats — someone else's boat

Worcester Polytechnic's SailBot entry, built from published competition figures rather than CAD. It earns its place by being different: a freely rotating rigid wingsail with a trim tab, not a soft sail, and two canted rudders on one servo.

That difference is what makes it a useful test of the slot design. Its config picks the basic sail on purpose — a symmetric NACA section is much closer to a rigid wing than the ORC envelope is, and the config says so in a comment. It offers no orc_main or orc_w_jib block at all, because those are soft-sail VPP coefficients and would be the wrong physics for this boat rather than merely an untuned one. The twin rudders are lumped into a single blade of the combined area, keeping the aspect ratio of one blade, since two blades side by side shed tip vortices like each other rather than like one wing of twice the area.

basic_sailbot — the default

The boat everything defaults to, and the one the shipped RL checkpoints were trained against. A 3 m² sail on a 0.75 m² keel is a generous rig by any real standard; it sails easily and forgivingly, which is what you want when the point is to check that the controls respond.

It is also the most useful boat for the swap experiment in section 07, because it offers three sail models and both keel and rudder models, so you can change one slot at a time and watch the handling change under you.

fun_boat — the same hull, sailed harder

Geometrically identical to basic_sailbot. It exists as a place to push numbers around without disturbing the boat the checkpoints were trained on — the configuration you edit when you want to see what happens, rather than the one you keep stable.

real_boat — the small-rig case

Same hull again, but with the sail cut to 1.34 m² and the keel to 0.13 m² — much closer to the proportions of an actual 1.5 m sailbot, and much harder to sail. Its yaw inertia of 1.0 kg·m² is the lowest of the five, so it spins up quickly and punishes clumsy steering. Despite the name it is not measured; Flingo is the measured one.

07

The models

The slots, and what fits in them

Each part of the boat is a slot. Several models compete for each, and a boat config picks one:

SlotModels offeredWhat the choice changes
sailbasic, hybrid, orc_main, orc_w_jibA NACA section polar, a flat-plate analytic form, or the ORC VPP coefficient envelope for a mainsail or a full sloop rig.
keelbasic, finite_spanA 2-D section polar, or lifting-line induced drag with a post-stall blend.
rudderbasic, finite_spanSame choice again — the rudder is a foil like any other.
hullbasic, linear, quadraticGeometry-derived drag with wave-making and added mass, or the two simpler hydro models.
frictionflat, hughesA constant skin-friction coefficient, or the Reynolds-dependent ITTC line. Composed into the hull, not inherited by it.
Why friction is its own slot

It is the clearest example of the rule this codebase now follows. The obvious way to add the Hughes friction line is a MeasuredHull subclass that overrides the friction method — and that was tried. The trouble is that a subclass fixes a combination: it then has to refuse a friction_model key, because it has already decided. Making friction a slot instead means any friction law composes with any hull, and the shipyard screen can offer the choice as something you click. Prefer a new model in a slot over a subclass that hardcodes a pairing.

A model registers itself

Nothing central holds a list of what exists. A model says what it is, and that is the only place it is written down:

@register("keel", "finite_span", name="Finite span",
          blurb="Lifting-line induced drag and post-stall blend")
class FiniteSpanKeel(BasicKeel):
    REQUIRES = (("span", "effective_aspect_ratio"),)   # one of these must be present
    REFUSES  = ()                                      # keys this model does not read
    DEFAULTS = {"alpha_sep_deg": 25.0}                 # physics constants it owns

name and blurb are what the boat-assembly screen shows, so a new model arrives already described. REQUIRES and REFUSES let that screen grey out a model a given boat cannot run, with the reason, without building anything — and the model enforces the same list at construction, so the screen and the boat cannot disagree.

Registration happens on import. A model in a file nobody imports is a model the registry has never heard of.

How a config names one

Shared keys apply whichever model runs; each model’s own parameters sit in a block it owns, so no model is ever handed another’s keys:

keel:
  area: 0.1225          # shared: every keel model reads these
  x_pos: 0.184
  model: finite_span    # which one to build
  models:
    basic: {}           # empty is fine
    finite_span:
      span: 0.700
      end_plate_factor: 2.0

A section with no models: block is read exactly as before, so older configs keep working and a boat migrates one part at a time.

Sail

The slot with the most in it, because a sail is the part the simulator finds hardest and the part that differs most between boats.

basic — symmetric section polar

Asks NeuralFoil for the lift and drag of a NACA section at a geometric angle of attack, then fades lift to zero below luff_deg so the sail stops working when the wind comes too head-on. It is the wrong shape for a soft cambered sail — a symmetric section makes no lift at zero incidence, where a real sail makes plenty — and it is exactly right for a rigid wingsail, which is why WPI's boat chooses it. Refuses the seven ORC-only keys.

hybrid — analytic flat plate

No polar lookup at all: CL = CL_max · sin(2α), which is the classic flat-plate result, with drag rising as 1 − cos(2α). A wing at small angles and a parachute at large ones. It stays sensible at the high angles sails actually work at, needs no foil data, and carries its own defaults (CL_max 1.2, CD0 0.1, CD1 1.0) so a boat can select it with an empty block.

orc_main — the ORC envelope, single mainsail

Coefficients from the Offshore Racing Congress VPP, tabulated against apparent wind angle rather than a geometric angle in a rotating frame, with drive and heel resolved in one explicit step — which makes a whole class of chained-rotation sign errors impossible to write. Sheet trim enters through ORC's flat parameter, so luffing falls out of the model instead of needing a bolted-on ramp. Refuses jib_area and the section-polar keys.

orc_w_jib — the ORC envelope, sloop

ORC's collective rig: the main and jib tables are blended by area share, so the rig points better and runs slower than a main-only rig of the same area. Requires jib_area — a sloop config cannot quietly run as a single sail, because the model refuses to build without it.

Keel

basic — 2-D section polar

A section polar evaluated at the leeway angle. Its blurb is honest about the consequence: side force comes almost for free. A 2-D polar has no tip to shed a vortex from, so the keel generates lift without paying the induced drag a real fin pays, and measured leeway comes out close to zero.

finite_span — lifting line and a stall blend

Charges the induced drag the lift actually costs: CL₃ₐ = CL₂ₐ · AR/(AR+2), with the drag penalty going as CL²/(π·AR·e). Past stall it blends toward a flat plate rather than clamping, so there is no kink in the derivative exactly where a stalled foil operates. Requires span or effective_aspect_ratio; defaults alpha_sep_deg to 25. An end_plate_factor handles a root sealed against the hull, which mirrors the flow and doubles the effective aspect ratio.

Rudder

The same two models as the keel — a rudder is a foil like any other — with one difference worth knowing.

basic — clamped past stall

Section polar with the coefficients clamped once the angle of attack goes past the table. Simple, and it pins the coefficients at a constant exactly where a hard-over rudder spends its time.

finite_span — blend instead of clamps

Induced drag and the same smooth separation blend as the keel. Because the blend replaces the clamps, this model refuses the three clamp keys rather than ignoring them — a config carrying both is telling the simulator two different things, and it says so instead of silently picking one.

Hull

basic — geometry

Quadratic drag derived from the hull's own dimensions, plus wave-making resistance so the boat has a real top speed instead of accelerating indefinitely, plus added mass integrated from a sections table when the boat supplies one. Requires L, B and T. Skin friction is not its business — that comes from the friction slot.

linear — linear viscous damping

Skips geometry entirely and takes its damping straight from xu1, yv1 and nr1 — drag proportional to speed rather than to speed squared. The simplest hull in the repository, and the easiest to reason about when you want a boat that behaves exactly as specified rather than as derived.

quadratic — quadratic viscous damping

The same idea one power up: xu2, yv2 and nr2, with drag going as speed squared, which is the right shape for a hull moving fast enough to make waves. Useful for a boat whose damping was measured directly rather than computed from its shape — and, like linear, for a regression test that should not move when the geometry model changes.

Friction

A slot rather than part of the hull, for the reason set out above: it lets any friction law compose with any hull instead of one subclass fixing the pair. A friction model is not a Model at all — it is a plain function of speed, waterline length and the hull's parameters, which is all the contract this slot needs.

flat — constant 0.004

A plausible mid-range coefficient that does not vary with speed — which is the one thing skin friction is known to do. It is what every config got before a law could be named, and it remains the honest default for a hull nobody has measured.

hughes — ITTC line with a form factor

Cf = 0.066 / (log₁₀Re − 2.03)², with Reynolds built on 85% of the waterline because the boundary layer does not run the full length, times a form factor because a hull is not a flat plate. Falls back to the flat coefficient below Re 10⁴, where the line does not hold.

Try it

All of this is selectable from the shipyard screen without editing a file. What RL is

Ordinary machine learning learns from labelled examples: here are photos, here are their labels. Sailing has no answer key — nobody can tell you the correct rudder angle for every situation.

Reinforcement learning instead learns from consequences. The agent acts, receives a numerical reward, and adjusts to get more of it. Like training a dog with treats, except the dog gets several hundred thousand attempts.

The task

A waypoint is dropped 12–25 m away and the boat must get within 1.5 m of it. With upwind_waypoint_bias: 1 the target is placed upwind on every single episode — the hard case, the one that requires tacking.

Two details in the observation are worth noticing. Everything is boat-relative and scaled, because neural networks learn far better on small, consistently-scaled inputs. And the previous action is fed back in, which gives the network a scrap of memory so it can produce smooth control rather than chattering the helm.

The reward function is where the engineering lives

Training uses PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization, from Stable-Baselines3. The core idea is in the name: proximal means stay nearby. When updating the network it clips how far the new behaviour may move from the old, about 20% here. Unconstrained RL updates are notoriously capable of destroying a good policy in a single bad batch; PPO trades some speed for not falling off cliffs, which is why it is the default choice in practice.

How training really goes

The short version, in one paragraph.

You start with a neural network full of random numbers, which means a boat that does nothing useful — it spins, it stalls, it wanders off. The computer runs that hopeless boat through the simulator over and over, and after every attempt it scores how well the boat did. Then it nudges the random numbers a tiny bit in whatever direction would have scored slightly higher. One nudge changes almost nothing. A million nudges, stacked on top of each other, gradually turn the random numbers into something that sails. Nobody ever tells the network the right answer, because nobody knows it. The only thing it ever receives is a score, and it has to work backwards from that to figure out what it should have done.

What actually happens when you press enter

You run the training script and your laptop goes quiet for several hours. Here is what it's doing in that time.

It opens four copies of the simulator at once, in four separate processes, so it can use four of your CPU cores instead of one. Each copy drops a waypoint upwind and lets the boat try to reach it. A single attempt — an episode — runs for up to 1,750 simulated steps, which is 35 seconds of boat-time, and ends early if the boat reaches the mark or drifts hopelessly far away.

Every 1,024 steps the trainer pauses, looks at everything that just happened, and updates the network. Then it goes again. It repeats this until it has seen a million steps, which for this run took roughly 5.5 hours of simulated sailing, compressed into a few hours of real time because the simulator runs far faster than real life.

Every 10,000 steps it stops and runs a proper exam: ten fresh episodes with the randomness switched off, to measure how good the policy honestly is right now. That exam is what produces the graph below — and it saves a copy of the network each time, so if training later goes wrong you still have the good version.

The real curve from this repository

This is not an illustration. It is the actual evaluation log from runs/good_vmg_stable/, the trained policy that ships with the repo — 100 exams taken over a million training steps.

Why it gets worse before it gets better

Look at the first stretch. The score starts around 240, and by 90,000 steps it has fallen to 110. Training made the boat worse. This is normal and it catches everyone out the first time.

The reason is that a brand-new random network isn't really doing anything — it wiggles the controls more or less at random, the boat drifts, and drifting happens to score mildly badly rather than terribly. As soon as the network starts having actual opinions, it commits to them, and its early opinions are wrong. Committing to a wrong idea scores worse than having no idea at all. It has to get through that before it can find a good idea.

The practical lesson: do not kill a training run because the first twenty minutes look bad. Judge it at 200,000 steps, not 20,000.

The part nobody tells you: you will do this many times

The graph above is a success. It is also not the first attempt. Look at the folder names sitting in runs/:

claude_run_4_jibe_penalty
claude_run_5_lr1e4_oscillating
good_vmg_stable

That is a lab notebook. Someone added a penalty for jibing and retrained. Someone tried a different learning rate and the policy oscillated — steering left, right, left, right, never settling. Only the third name says "stable."

This is what real RL work is actually like, and it's the part that surprises people who expect it to be about neural networks. Most of the time goes into the reward function, not the network. You write down what you think "sailing well" means, train for hours, watch the result, and discover the agent found a loophole — it spins in circles because turning scored points, or it parks downwind because the penalty for being in the no-go zone outweighed the reward for arriving. Then you rewrite the reward and go again.

The core difficulty

An agent optimises exactly what you wrote down, never what you meant. If there is a cheaper way to score points than actually sailing well, it will find it, and it will find it faster than you expect. Every penalty term in rl_waypoint_sb3.yaml — the jibe penalty, the no-go-zone penalty, the penalty for twitching the controls — exists because somebody watched an agent exploit its absence.

Watching it happen

You do not have to stare at a terminal. Two options:

  • TensorBoard — a web dashboard that draws the score curve live as training runs. You launch it in a second terminal and refresh a browser tab. This is how you spot a run going wrong at 100,000 steps instead of discovering it four hours later.
  • The --watch-web flag — streams one of the four training boats into the same 3D browser view you use to sail manually. Watching the boat learn is genuinely useful for diagnosis: an oscillating policy is obvious in three seconds of video and nearly invisible in a score graph.

Where sailbench sits in the real system

Everything above trains a policy that is excellent at sailing this simulator. The boat, meanwhile, already has a full software stack of its own — and sailbench is currently outside it.

The boat's Jetson runs ROS 2 Humble. A VectorNav sensor node publishes GPS position and heading. A Teensy microcontroller reads the anemometer, drives the actuators, and publishes wind and actuator telemetry. A navigation node turns position, heading, wind and waypoint into rudder commands, while a separate sail-trimming node decides the sail angle. A multiplexer chooses between the autonomous algorithm, the radio controller, the ground-station webserver and a controller app before anything reaches the hardware.

The three gaps that matter most

1. The real boat has two sails. The Teensy drives a rudder, a mainsail, and two jib servos. Sailbench models a single sail with a single sheet command, and the RL policy's action vector has exactly two numbers in it. A policy trained here has no concept of a jib, and the real boat's sail-trimming is a separate node from its navigation — a split the simulator does not have either.

2. The anemometer measures apparent wind. The policy's observation is built from the config's true wind. A masthead sensor cannot give you that; it gives you apparent. This is not a small offset — the error grows with boatspeed, exactly when it matters. Either convert apparent to true onboard, or retrain with apparent wind in the observation, which is the cleaner fix because apparent is what you can actually measure.

3. The simulator's known wrongness. Section 12 lists it: two-dimensional so the boat never heels, no waves, and a single constant wind. A policy trained against those assumptions has quietly learned to depend on them.

The standard remedy for the third is domain randomisation: instead of one wind speed, randomise the wind, gusts and shifts, the boat's mass, sensor noise and control latency on every episode. A policy that survives all of that has been forced to learn something robust rather than something that exploits one set of numbers. Sailbench randomises the starting position, heading and waypoint already — but the wind and boat parameters are fixed, which is the obvious next step.

RL is not replacing anything yet

The boat already sails using a deterministic algorithm — hand-written rules, currently Python ROS 2 nodes on the Jetson, being ported to standalone C++ so the long-range boat can navigate without a Jetson at all. The plan is not to swap it for RL. It is to build a fair comparison: run both through identical scenarios and identical metrics, and let the results decide. That makes the deterministic controller the baseline the RL policy has to beat, which is a much healthier framing than treating the learned policy as the goal in itself.

It also gives sailbench a second job it cannot do today: testing the deterministic algorithm under wind, weather and failure conditions that are unsafe or impractical to reproduce on a real lake. That job needs the ROS 2 link, not more training.

Testing against real code and hardware

Two programs, two languages, one laptop, no hardware at all — and the operating system already provides the wire between them.

Before any hardware is involved, you can test the navigation algorithm against the simulator on one laptop, with no board, no cable and no radio. This is where most bugs die, and it is worth setting up properly because it stays useful for the rest of the project.

The obstacle looks like a language problem: the Teensy code is C++, sailbench is Python. They cannot call each other directly. But they do not need to — they can run as two completely independent programs that pass messages, and the operating system already provides the channel.

What a pipe is

A pipe is a one-way channel between two running programs: one writes bytes in, the other reads them out, and the operating system holds whatever is in transit. It is exactly the mechanism behind the | in a shell command like ls | grep foo, where one program's output becomes another's input.

One pipe carries one direction, so a conversation needs two. The tidiest way to get both is to let Python launch the C++ program as a child process and take hold of its standard input and standard output — which are a pair of pipes already. Python writes a sensor line to the child's stdin; the child writes a command line back on its stdout.

Wiring it up

Python's subprocess is in the standard library, so this needs no new dependency at all — unlike the serial stage, which needs pyserial:

import subprocess

algo = subprocess.Popen(
    ["./nav_algo"],              # the C++ binary, built for your laptop
    stdin=subprocess.PIPE,
    stdout=subprocess.PIPE,
    text=True,                   # talk in strings, not raw bytes
    bufsize=1,                   # line buffered
)

while True:
    algo.stdin.write(sensor_line(state, hub, t) + "\n")
    algo.stdin.flush()                              # <- do not forget this

    cmd = parse(algo.stdout.readline())             # blocks until B replies

    state = hub.step(state, dt, rk4_step,
                     sail_angle=radians(cmd.sheet_deg),
                     rudder_angle=cmd.rudder_deg)
    t += dt

And the C++ side is about as plain as code gets — no sockets, no libraries, nothing platform-specific:

std::string line;
while (std::getline(std::cin, line)) {
    Sensors s = parse_sensor_line(line);
    Command c = navigate(s);                        // the real algorithm
    std::cout << "C " << c.rudder_deg << " " << c.sheet_deg << std::endl;
}
The one bug everybody hits: buffering deadlock

When a program's output goes to a terminal it is flushed line by line, but when it goes to a pipe the C runtime switches to holding several kilobytes before sending anything. So the C++ program computes its answer, writes it — and the text sits in a buffer that never reaches Python. Python waits forever for a reply; the C++ program waits forever for the next input. Nothing crashes and no error appears. Both programs simply hang.

The fix is one habit on each side. In C++, end every reply with std::endl rather than "\n" (endl flushes), or call setvbuf(stdout, nullptr, _IONBF, 0) once at startup to switch buffering off entirely. In Python, pass bufsize=1 and call .flush() after every write. If your very first attempt hangs with no output, this is why — it is almost never anything else.

Why plain text lines

One line of space-separated values per message, with a single letter saying which kind it is. It needs no parser library on either side, it survives being read by a human, and — most usefully — you can run the C++ program on its own and type sensor lines at it by hand to see what it does. A format you can drive from a keyboard is a format you can debug.

What you get, and what you do not

This arrangement is lockstep by construction: Python writes, then blocks until B answers. There is no clock and no real time anywhere in it, which makes every run exactly reproducible — the same scenario produces the same track, every time. That is what makes it suitable for the test suite, and it means you can run it much faster than real time and sweep a hundred wind conditions in the time one lake test would take.

It is also a normal desktop program, so all the ordinary tools work: a debugger, breakpoints, printf, and sanitiser builds that catch memory errors before they turn into an unexplained reboot on the water.

What it cannot tell you is anything about time. Lockstep politely waits for a slow loop, so an algorithm far too slow for the Teensy passes happily. Nor does it test memory limits, the single-precision FPU, or the serial framing. Those are exactly what the hardware stages are for — which is the argument for doing this stage first and briefly, not instead.

Where this sits

This is the first and cheapest rung of the testing ladder. The next section puts the same navigation code on the real microcontroller, where a pipe becomes a serial cable and the code under test does not change. Do this stage first and briefly — not instead of it.

Hardware in the loop

How you test navigation code that runs on a microcontroller without putting a boat in a lake — and the specific shape this takes for a Teensy-resident algorithm.

What it is

Hardware in the loop means running the real embedded code on the real microcontroller, but feeding it simulated sensor readings and capturing its actuator commands back into the simulator instead of driving real servos. The control loop still closes — it just closes through physics running on a laptop rather than through water.

The board genuinely cannot tell the difference. It reads what it thinks is a GPS fix, computes a rudder angle, and writes it out, exactly as it would on the water. What changes is only what is on the other end of the wire.

What runs where today

Before any of the rest makes sense, it helps to be exact about which box reads which sensor and which box decides anything. There are two computers on this boat, and the division of labour is not symmetric.

That last row is the whole story. Wind enters through the microcontroller; position and heading enter through the Jetson. The two halves of what a navigator needs arrive at opposite ends of the boat, and everything about the Teensy port follows from that.

Who decides anything

The Jetson runs ROS 2 Humble, and essentially all the intelligence lives there as separate nodes: the deterministic navigation algorithm (position + heading + wind + waypoint → rudder command), a separate sail-trimming node that decides sail angle, a multiplexer that chooses between the autonomous algorithm, the radio controller, the ground-station webserver and a controller app, event nodes for waypoints and competition behaviours such as station keeping, vision nodes, and ROSBridge linking all of it to the ground station.

The Teensy runs firmware, not ROS. It reads the anemometer, drives the actuators, reports wind, actuator and dropped-packet telemetry back, and accepts commands from either the Jetson or the radio controller. What it does not receive is GPS, heading or waypoints — so today it cannot make a navigation decision even in principle. It is an I/O board with a radio failsafe.

The actuators all hang off the Teensy: the rudder servo, the mainsail winch, and two jib servos.

Where the algorithm is going

Which raises a fork worth deciding early. For the long-range boat to be genuinely Jetson-less, the VectorNav has to reach the Teensy without the Jetson in the path — a wiring and firmware change, not just a software one. If instead the Jetson keeps supplying position, the algorithm has moved but the dependency has not, and the power and fault-tolerance argument for the port largely evaporates.

Why this matters specifically for the Teensy port

The plan is to move the navigation algorithm off the Jetson and onto the Teensy as standalone C++, so the long-range boat can navigate without a Jetson, ROS 2, or the link between them at all. That is a substantial win for power and fault tolerance — and it moves the algorithm from a machine where debugging is easy onto one where it is genuinely hard: no operating system, limited memory, no convenient logging, and a watchdog that reboots you if a loop runs long.

Hardware in the loop is where that new data path gets designed and shaken out — iterating in seconds, against a simulator you can restart and feed any value you like, instead of in lake trips.

The design rule that makes it worth doing

The firmware must not know

Sailbench should impersonate the Jetson, speaking the protocol the Teensy already expects, so the firmware needs no HIL-specific code, no build flag, no test mode. The moment the board behaves differently under test than in flight, you are no longer testing the thing you ship.

This is also convenient, because the protocol has to be extended anyway to carry GPS, heading and waypoints to the Teensy. Designing that extension against a simulator — where you can restart in a second and inject any value you like — is far easier than designing it against a boat.

What sailbench needs for this

Less than it sounds. web_runner.py is already a loop that drives SailboatHub, takes control inputs from an external source, and streams state out. A HIL runner is the same shape with a serial port where the WebSocket is:

  • Open a serial port instead of a socket.
  • Each step, build a sensor frame from the sim state and write it.
  • Read the actuator frame back and feed it in as rudder and sheet commands.
  • Step the physics. Optionally also stream to the browser, so you can watch the boat your firmware believes it is sailing.

SailboatHub itself needs no changes at all — which is the payoff for having kept the physics core independent of its front end.

Real time or lockstep — support both

The Teensy runs in real time; the simulator normally runs as fast as it can. Two ways to reconcile that, and they catch different things:

  • Free-running real time. Sailbench paces itself to the wall clock, as the web runner already does. Faithful to real timing, so it exposes jitter and overruns — but not exactly repeatable.
  • Lockstep. The board requests a step, the sim advances exactly one dt, sends state, and waits. Perfectly deterministic and reproducible — ideal for regression tests — but it hides timing bugs by definition, because the simulator politely waits for a slow loop.

Use lockstep for the test suite and free-running for validating that the thing actually keeps up.

Three Teensy-specific things to plan for

  • Single-precision arithmetic. Teensy 4.x is a Cortex-M7 with a hardware FPU that is single-precision; double is emulated in software and slow, so the C++ will almost certainly use float. Python and NumPy are double throughout. A trig-heavy navigation algorithm can drift measurably between the two, and HIL is how you find out whether that drift matters before it matters on the water.
  • The anemometer lives on the Teensy. The board reads wind directly, so the HIL layer must synthesise that reading — and it must synthesise apparent wind, because that is what a masthead sensor measures. This is the same true-versus-apparent distinction that bites the RL observation vector in section 10.
  • Coordinates. The sim works in local metres; the navigation code expects GPS latitude and longitude from the VectorNav. The HIL layer has to project between them about a reference point — the same projection the real-lake import in section 13 would need, so building it once serves both.

What HIL catches, and what it cannot

It is very good at a class of bug that pure simulation simply cannot reach: a loop that overruns its budget, an integer that overflows at high latitude, a serial frame that is misparsed when two arrive back to back, a watchdog that fires during a long computation, a controller that oscillates because the servo cannot slew fast enough. It also lets you inject failures deliberately — drop the GPS for ten seconds, corrupt a packet, saturate an actuator — and watch what the boat does, which is exactly the experiment you cannot safely run on a lake.

The honest limit

Hardware in the loop validates your code against your model, never against reality. If the simulator has no heel, no waves, no induced drag and one sail where the boat has two, then a green HIL run tells you the firmware correctly sails a boat that does not exist. It raises confidence in the software and says nothing about the physics. Those are separate problems, fixed by separate work — sections 06 and 12.

Properties of the model, not bugs

These set the ceiling on how far any result here can be trusted.

  • It is two-dimensional. No heeling, no waves. Real sailboats lean over, which spills wind and changes the keel’s effective area — an entire feedback loop the simulator does not have and no policy trained here has ever seen. The ORC sail can depower against a righting-moment limit, which is the nearest thing available in 3 DOF, but it is not heel.
  • One constant wind. No gusts, no shifts, no shoreline effects. You can change the wind live from the browser, which is the manual version, but nothing randomises it during training.
  • Reynolds numbers are configured, not computed. The section-polar models look up coefficients at a Reynolds number the config states, rather than one derived from the speed at that instant. The measured boat states a value derived from its own chords, which is much better than a round guess, but it is still fixed.

  • HybridSail ignores its own config. LUFF_DEG = 5.0 is a hardcoded module constant, and the model never reads luff_deg from the YAML at all — so a config setting it is silently discarded. Two sources of truth, and the config is the one that loses.

1 · Unit tests — what they are, and the seven that fail

A unit test is a small function that calls one piece of your code with known inputs and asserts something about the result. Run the whole suite in a couple of seconds and you learn whether a change broke anything, without launching the simulator and squinting at a boat.

The suite here is real and now large — 381 tests covering the foils, the hull and friction models, the registry, the transform tree, the shipyard catalogue and the RL environment. It used to be 48 with seven failing on a clean clone, which is corrosive in a specific way: a permanently red suite trains everyone to ignore red, and once that happens it cannot be used as a gate at all. That is no longer the situation, which is what made the gate possible.

The failing tests had to be fixed before the gate went up. Turning on a check that everyone already knows is broken just teaches the team to click past it. Two were stale test fixtures, one was an assertion comparing zero to zero, and one was a genuine sign-convention bug in the rudder — that last needed a decision, not a patch, and it was settled by giving each component its own flow rotation instead of sharing one frame.

2–3 · Continuous integration — what it is

CI means that every time someone opens a pull request, a fresh machine somewhere checks out the branch, installs the dependencies, and runs the tests and the linter automatically. If anything fails, GitHub puts a red mark on the PR and — once configured — refuses to let it merge.

The point isn't automation for its own sake. It is that "it works on my machine" stops being an argument. Right now nothing prevents merging a branch that breaks the physics, because nobody is obliged to run pytest first. CI makes that obligation automatic and impersonal: the robot says no, not a teammate.

A linter is a separate tool that reads code without running it and flags style problems and likely-bug patterns — unused imports, missing annotations, shadowed variables. ruff is the one this project already declares. Gating means the PR cannot merge until both checks pass.

What needs to be done

This could not land in one step, because switching ruff on as configured would have failed every PR. Two reasons, in order — both now fixed, and recorded here because the reasoning is the reusable part:

  • The lint config sits in a deprecated location. select = ["ALL"] is under [tool.ruff], but modern ruff wants [tool.ruff.lint]. Ruff prints a warning about it on every single run.
  • "ALL" means literally every rule, including mutually contradictory ones. That was 923 violations on the current tree, and most were noise rather than bugs: 78 are S101 (use of assert — flagged by the security ruleset, but asserts are how pytest works), 37 are "magic value in comparison" firing on every numeric assertion in the tests, 27 are import-order complaints about a deliberate sys.path shuffle in scripts/.

So the order is: move select under [tool.ruff.lint] and narrow it to a rule set the team agrees with, add per-file exemptions so test-only rules don't fire in test/, run the autofixer, clear what's left, and only then add the workflow file and turn on branch protection. Add mypy last — pyproject.toml sets strict = true, which will surface plenty, and that shouldn't block the tests-and-lint gate from landing.

4 · Coverage — what it is

Line coverage is the percentage of source lines that executed at least once while the suite ran. You measure it with a tool that watches which lines fire:

The term-missing part is the useful half — it prints the line numbers that never ran, so you get a to-do list rather than a grade.

It is not proof of correctness. A line can execute and still be wrong — every one of the seven failures that used to sit in this repo was in code that was 100% "covered." Coverage tells you where you are definitely not looking. It says nothing about how carefully you looked at the rest. Treat it as a floor, never a target to game.

5 · ROS 2 integration — the one the team has committed to

Everything above is hygiene — making the repository safe to work in. This one is the stated objective for the semester, and it is the item that changes what sailbench is for. It does not depend on the chain above, so it starts now.

ROS 2 is the framework the boat runs on — the standard robotics middleware, where each component is a separate program (a node) and they communicate by publishing and subscribing to named channels (topics). The Jetson runs ROS 2 Humble, and every major navigation component is a node on it.

Sailbench is not one of those nodes. It is a standalone Python process that talks to a browser over its own WebSocket. So today the simulator cannot test the code that actually sails the boat — it can only test its own internal RL environment. Joining the two would let the real navigation algorithm drive a simulated boat, which is how you test a controller against gusts, wind shifts, sensor dropouts and actuator failures that are unsafe or impractical to stage on a lake.

Concretely, that means sailbench publishing what the boat's sensors publish — GPS position, heading, wind, actuator telemetry — and subscribing to the rudder and sail commands the navigation nodes emit, so that from the algorithm's point of view the simulator is indistinguishable from the hardware.

Why this comes before the RL work

The end goal is a fair comparison between the learned policy and the existing deterministic algorithm, on identical scenarios with identical metrics. That comparison is impossible until the deterministic algorithm can run inside sailbench at all — and it cannot, today. Every RL improvement is worth less until there is something honest to measure it against.

6 · The sail is modelled as an aircraft wing

This one is about accuracy rather than process, and it is the simulator's most questionable physics assumption.

BasicSail asks NeuralFoil for the lift and drag of a NACA0012 section — a rigid, symmetric aircraft wing, 12% as thick as it is long. A sail is none of those things.

The tell: luffing is bolted on rather than emergent

A real sail luffs because a membrane cannot carry a reversed pressure difference. It falls out of the physics. In basic_sail.py it is instead applied afterwards, with two hand-tuned numbers chosen to make the output look plausible. When a model needs a bolted-on correction for something it should produce naturally, that is the model telling you it has the wrong shape.

A better-shaped model already exists here and is not wired up

hybrid_sail.py uses an analytic form instead of a foil lookup: cl = cl_max × sin(2α). Since sin(2α) is 2·sin(α)·cos(α), that is the classic flat-plate result — a far better starting point for a thin membrane than a thick airfoil section, and it stays sensible at the high angles sails actually work at. But the hub instantiates BasicSail; HybridSail is imported and never used.

What needs to be done

First decide which model is canonical and delete or clearly mark the other. Two sail models where the tested one is not the running one is worse than having either alone. Then improve the survivor, roughly in this order:

  • Add camber. The single biggest accuracy win — it makes the sail generate lift at zero angle and behave asymmetrically about it, which is how sails actually work. Make it a config parameter.
  • Make luffing emergent. Collapse the lift when the pressure difference reverses, not when an angle crosses a threshold. The magic numbers then disappear on their own.
  • Blend two regimes. Thin-airfoil theory at small angles into the flat-plate form at large ones. Sails spend real time in both.
  • Compute Reynolds from actual apparent-wind speed instead of the hardcoded res: [1e5].
  • Later, model mainsail and jib separately. The real boat has a mainsail and two jib servos; the RL action vector has one sheet command. This is a prerequisite for anything that transfers to hardware.

How to tell whether it got better

scripts/polar_diagram.py already exists. A polar diagram — boatspeed plotted against wind angle — is how sailors have always characterised a boat, and it makes model errors obvious: the no-go zone should be a clean notch, speed should peak on a reach, and there should be a visible dip dead downwind. Generate one before and after any change.

Do not judge this by the RL score

A more accurate sail model will probably make scores drop at first, because the current policy was trained against the old model and has learned to exploit its specific wrongness. That is the sim-to-real gap appearing in miniature — and it is the whole reason this work matters.

7 · The web UI

Last because it blocks nothing — but the first tier is close to free, and two items directly serve the RL-versus-deterministic comparison that items 5 and 6 are building toward.

The UI currently has zero inputs, buttons or sliders. The only interactive element in web/index.html is the forces-panel header. Keyboard input is the entire control surface.

Tier 1 — the backend already supports these; the UI never sends them

apply_controls() handles wind_speed, wind_dir_deg, paused and reset. main.js sends none of them. This tier is wiring, not building.

  • Wind controls. A speed slider and a draggable compass dial. This single control turns the sim from a demo into a test rig — and it is the manual version of the domain randomisation on the mid-fall research list.
  • Pause, reset, single-step. Add step while you are there: advancing one 20 ms tick at a time is how you actually debug a force sign error like the rudder bug.
  • VMG readout. It is what the RL reward optimises, and the HUD does not show it. Computable client-side from data already being sent.

Tier 2 — highest value for the comparison work

  • A boat track. You cannot see where the boat has been. A persistent trail with tack points marked is the single most useful addition for comparing the two algorithms — two tracks overlaid, same wind, same waypoint, is the comparison.
  • Rolling strip charts. Speed, heading and rudder over the last 30 seconds. Recall claude_run_5_lr1e4_oscillating: the oscillation was invisible in the score but would be unmistakable as a sawtooth. Instantaneous bars cannot show a behaviour.
  • True and apparent wind as separate arrows. Given the true/apparent mismatch in the observation vector, drawing both turns an invisible bug class into something you can see. The sim computes apparent wind internally and discards it.
  • The no-go zone on the water. A translucent wedge anchored to the boat, rotating with the wind. The constraint the whole task is built around is currently invisible.

Tier 3 — build this one first anyway

A live reward-decomposition panel. _build_info() already computes reward_vmg, penalty_no_go_zone, penalty_jibe, penalty_joint and penalty_stagnation — every term, every step — and none of it reaches the browser. It is thrown away.

Show it as a live stacked bar: positive contributions up, penalties down, net in the middle. When the boat does something stupid you can see which term paid for it, in the moment. Section 10 makes the case that most RL time goes into the reward function rather than the network — this is the instrument for that work, and the data already exists.

Tier 4 — the sailing-native one

Telltales. Real sailors trim by watching ribbons taped to the sail: streaming aft means attached flow, fluttering means stalled or luffing. Angle of attack is already computed every step — drive a few animated telltales from it.

It is charming, but also functional in a way a number is not: it makes luffing visible as behaviour rather than a HUD value. It is the natural companion to item 6 — as camber and emergent luffing go in, the telltales become the readout for whether the new model behaves like a sail.

Tier 5 — bigger builds

  • Ghost replay. A recorded episode as a translucent boat alongside a live one. Best-run-versus-current is how you see whether a change actually helped.
  • Live polar diagram. scripts/polar_diagram.py already generates these offline. Put one in the corner with a dot for the current operating point against the theoretical curve. A sailor reads that instantly: am I fast for this angle, or leaving speed on the table?
  • Scenario picker with a fixed seed. The late-fall deliverable asks for repeatable evaluation scenarios. A dropdown loading a named scenario with fixed wind and seed makes "run both algorithms on scenario 3" a UI action rather than a code edit.

Tier 6 — sailing on a real lake

Import a real shoreline and sail inside it. Worth taking seriously, with two caveats.

Use OpenStreetMap, not Google Maps. You want a shoreline polygon, not imagery. OSM tags lakes as natural=water and the Overpass API returns the coordinates free under ODbL. Google's terms prohibit extracting geometry or deriving datasets from their imagery — for a student project that gets published, that difference matters.

Split it in two, because the halves cost very differently. Phase 1 is geometry as scenery and boundary: load the polygon, project latitude and longitude to local metres, render it, ground the boat, end the episode on grounding. No observation change — the policy still cannot see land, but you can, and the deterministic algorithm can be tested against real geometry immediately. Days of work. Phase 2 makes the policy aware of it via a raycast fan, which roughly doubles the observation size, changes the network shape, and invalidates every existing checkpoint. A semester-scale project on its own.

The trap in tier 6

Accurate geometry with a uniform wind field can be worse than no geometry. Real wind near a shoreline shadows behind terrain, bends along the shore and gusts unpredictably — sailors know a shoreline as the place where wind becomes unreliable. Render a lake precisely, blow a constant 5 m/s across it, and the picture looks authoritative while the physics has become less representative in exactly the place the new constraint lives. Pair it with at least a crude rule reducing wind speed and adding directional noise near land.

VectorNavThe boat's navigation sensor: a combined GPS and inertial measurement unit, fused into one device. GPS alone gives position, but it updates slowly and can only tell you your course over ground — the direction you are actually travelling. The inertial half (accelerometers and gyroscopes) gives orientation many times a second. Fusing them yields position and true heading at a usable rate. Why that distinction matters here: a sailboat points one way and moves another, and the gap between the two is leeway — which is precisely the keel's angle of attack. A GPS course alone cannot see it; a real heading sensor can. It also reports roll and pitch, meaning the boat can already measure heel — the one motion sailbench has no variable for. On the boat it publishes into ROS 2 on the Jetson, which is why the Teensy does not currently see position or heading at all.
VMGVelocity Made Good — how fast you close on the target. Speed in the wrong direction counts for nothing.
WeathervaningA free sail swinging to align itself with the apparent wind.
XBeeA small radio module that provides a wireless serial link — you feed bytes in at one end and they come out the other, with the radio hidden. That transparency is the appeal: code written to talk over a wire needs no changes to talk over the air, which is why it pairs naturally with a microcontroller. On the boat it is the telemetry path to and from the ground station, carrying position, wind and actuator state out and waypoints or mode changes back. The tradeoff that matters for long-range sailing is range against bandwidth: you can reach much further by sending much less, so the link shapes what telemetry is affordable rather than the other way round. It is also worth knowing that a radio link drops packets, which is why the Teensy reports dropped-packet telemetry at all — and injecting that loss deliberately is one of the experiments hardware in the loop is for. On the team's project list, XBee work sits alongside connecting the VectorNav directly to the Teensy — which is exactly the new data path the Teensy port depends on.
YAML fileA plain-text format for writing down settings, designed to be readable by people rather than compact for machines. Indentation does the work that brackets do in other formats — nesting a name under another name by indenting it means "this belongs to that." A line like wind_speed: 5 is simply a name and a value. In sailbench, YAML is the tuning surface: configs/ holds one file per boat and one per training run, and changing sail area, wind, keel size, rudder position or every reinforcement-learning hyperparameter is a text edit with no Python involved. The trap: indentation is significant, so a stray space changes the meaning, and duplicate keys are silently resolved by keeping the last one — which is live in this repo right now, where the sail block in basic_sailbot.yaml defines alpha_min, alpha_max and res twice. Harmless only because the copies happen to match.
